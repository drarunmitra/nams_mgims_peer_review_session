1. COPE Ethical Guidelines for Peer Reviewers

Status of v2: NOT verified. The official PDF (v2, September 2017) and guidance page returned 403 to curl and WebFetch. A search-engine snippet says v2 is dated September 2017, expands v1 with material for early-career researchers and peer review fraud, and that later COPE page notes mention a 2020 branding refresh and a 2024 wording change ("anonymised review"). I could not open any of that. I did not find anything newer than v2 but could not check the COPE page itself. Do not put v2 wording on a slide until someone opens the official PDF in a browser.

What I did read: two mirror copies (car.ca, wnus.usz.edu.pl) that are both v1, March 2013, Irene Hames for COPE Council. v1 headings: "Basic principles to which peer reviewers should adhere"; "Expectations during the peer-review process" with sub-headings "On being approached to review", "During review", "When preparing the report"; "Expectations post review". v1 wording (v2 may differ):

  • Confidentiality: "respect the confidentiality of peer review and not reveal any details of a manuscript or its review, during or after the peer-review process, beyond those that are released by the journal". Also "keep all manuscript and review details confidential" and "not involve anyone else in the review of a manuscript ... without first obtaining permission from the journal".
  • Conflicts of interest: "declare all potential conflicting interests, seeking advice from the journal if they are unsure whether something constitutes a relevant interest". Default triggers if the journal gives no guidance: same institution as an author, recent mentor/mentee/close collaborator/joint grant holder (e.g. within the past 3 years), close personal relationship.
  • Timeliness: "only agree to review a manuscript if they are fairly confident they can return a review within the proposed or mutually agreed time-frame, informing the journal promptly if they require an extension." Also "respond in a reasonable time-frame, especially if they cannot do the review".
  • Competence: "only agree to review manuscripts for which they have the subject expertise required to carry out a proper assessment and which they can assess in a timely manner"; "notify the journal as soon as possible if they find they do not have the expertise to assess all aspects of the manuscript".
  • Constructive and professional tone: "be objective and constructive in their reviews and provide feedback that will help the authors to improve their manuscript"; "not make derogatory personal comments or unfounded accusations"; "be specific in their criticisms, and provide evidence"; "make clear which suggested additional investigations are essential to support claims ... and which will just strengthen or extend the work"; "be aware of the sensitivities surrounding language issues".
  • Also useful: confidential comments to the editor "should not be a place for denigration or false accusation".

2. ICMJE (opened in full; page footer shows 2026, no explicit revision date found on the page)

Section II.C.3 "Peer Reviewers" (full text on the page; AI sentences are new relative to older versions):

  • Confidentiality: "Reviewers therefore should keep manuscripts and the information they contain strictly confidential. Reviewers must not publicly discuss authors' work and must not appropriate authors' ideas before the manuscript is published. Reviewers must not retain the manuscript for their personal use and should destroy copies of manuscripts after submitting their reviews."
  • Helpers: "Reviewers who seek assistance from a trainee or colleague in the performance of a review should acknowledge these individuals' contributions in the written comments submitted to the editor."
  • AI: confidentiality "may prohibit the uploading of the manuscript to software or other AI technologies where confidentiality cannot be assured"; "Reviewers must request permission from the journal prior to using AI technology to facilitate their review."
  • Duties: "Reviewers are expected to respond promptly to requests to review and to submit reviews within the time agreed. Reviewers' comments should be constructive, honest, and polite."
  • Conflicts: "Reviewers should declare their relationships and activities that might bias their evaluation of a manuscript and recuse themselves from the peer-review process if a conflict exists."
  • II.C.2.a (journals): manuscripts are "privileged communications"; "Editors must also make clear that reviewers should keep manuscripts, associated material, and the information they contain strictly confidential."
  • II.C.2.c: peer review defined as "the critical assessment of manuscripts submitted to journals by experts who are usually not part of the editorial staff"; journals "should publish a clear, transparent description of their peer-review process".
  • Ethics reporting (IV.A, Methods) : "The Methods section should include a statement indicating that the research was approved by an independent local, regional or national review body (e.g., ethics committee, institutional review board)." II.E: authors "should seek approval to conduct research from an independent local, regional or national review body", Helsinki Declaration "as revised in 2024".
  • IV.A also: "Describe statistical methods with enough detail to enable a knowledgeable reader with access to the original data to judge its appropriateness" and quantify findings with "confidence intervals". Names STROBE for observational studies and EQUATOR as "Good sources for reporting guidelines".

3. Models of peer review (Ross-Hellauer 2017, full text)

  • Single-blind: "Traditional peer review operates as either 'single-blind', where authors do not know reviewers' identities, or 'double-blind', where both authors and reviewers remain anonymous." (Single-blind is "by far the most common model", per the paper citing Walker & Rocha da Silva 2015.)
  • Open peer review has no standard definition: 122 definitions, 22 configurations of 7 traits. Traits as defined: open identities ("Authors and reviewers are aware of each other's identity"); open reports ("Review reports are published alongside the relevant article"); open participation ("The wider community are able to contribute to the review process"); open interaction (reciprocal discussion between authors and reviewers allowed and encouraged); open pre-review manuscripts (preprints before formal review); open final-version commenting ("Review or commenting on final 'version of record' publications"); open platforms or "decoupled review". Open identities plus open reports cover 116 (95.1%) of definitions; adding open participation covers 121 (99.2%).
  • Pragmatic definition proposed: OPR "as an umbrella term for a number of overlapping ways that peer review models can be adapted in line with the aims of Open Science".
  • Post-publication review: no authoritative definition obtained. The paper says "post-publication peer review" is "closely-related" to OPR but excluded unless sources equate them; its nearest trait is open final-version commenting (above). I did not open COPE, publisher or other pages defining it. Slide should use the Ross-Hellauer trait wording or a source found later.

4. What reviewers detect

  • Schroter 2008 (abstract only): 607 BMJ peer reviewers randomised to face-to-face training, self-taught package, or control. Each got the same three test papers, each with 9 major and 5 minor inserted errors. Baseline (Paper 1) mean 2.58 of 9 major errors; Papers 2 and 3 means 2.71 and 3.0. "The interventions had small effects." Biased randomisation was most frequently detected, "over 60% of reviewers rejecting the papers identifying this error"; reviewers who did not reject found fewer errors and "the proportion finding biased randomization was less than 40% for each paper". Conclusion: "Editors should not assume that reviewers will detect most major errors". I did not see the minor-error numbers (not in the abstract).
  • Hopewell 2014 (full text; abstract numbers): 93 trial reports. Of these, 38% (n=35) did not describe random sequence generation, 54% (n=50) allocation concealment, 50% (n=46) blinding, 34% (n=32) sample size calculation, 35% (n=33) primary and secondary outcomes, 55% (n=51) results for the primary outcome, 90% (n=84) trial protocol details. Reviewer-requested changes were relatively few; positive examples: randomisation/blinding (n=27), sample size (n=15), outcomes (n=16), results (n=14), toning down conclusions (n=27); negative: extra unplanned analyses (n=15). These percentages describe what was missing in the submitted manuscripts (setting: BMC-series open-review journals).
  • Jefferson (Cochrane MR000016 pub3, 2007; abstract only): searches to June 2004; 28 studies. "no clear-cut evidence of effect" of reviewer/author concealment (9 studies); checklists "have some evidence to support their use (2 studies)"; "no evidence that referees' training has any effect" (1 study); peer review "appears to make papers more readable and improve the general quality of reporting (2 studies)" with limited generalisability. I did not check whether a newer version of MR000016 exists (PubMed shows pub3, 2007, as the record).

5. Structure and tone

  • Kelly 2014 (full text): ten "how to be an effective peer reviewer" points credited to Brian Lucey: be professional, pleasant ("do not leave ad hominem comments. There is no benefit to being ruthless"), read the invite, helpful ("Suggest how the authors can overcome the shortcomings"), scientific (do not fill the review with typographic comments), timely, realistic, empathetic, open, organised. Structure: "Begin with an overview of the proposed improvements; then provide feedback on the paper structure, the quality of data sources and methods of investigation used, the logical flow of argument, and the validity of conclusions drawn. Then provide feedback on style". Recommendation: reviewers "give recommendations to the editor regarding whether the paper should be accepted, rejected, or improved". The words "major" and "minor" comments do not appear in this sense. Also notes reviewers may "tone down their criticisms in order to be polite".
  • Stiller-Reeve 2018 (full text via copy): three readings; "classify your comments as major or minor flaws. Major flaws will need considerable time to explain or correct." A "fatal flaw" ends the review early. After reading one, "mirror" the article by writing your understanding of the science. Review template: "1. Introduction: Mirror the article, state your expertise and whether the paper is publishable, or whether there are fatal flaws; 2. Major flaws; 3. Minor flaws; 4. Other, lesser suggestions and final comments." Tone: read the review aloud; "Ensure that your critiques are constructive and not offensive. Be helpful and not harmful." Cites the "golden rule": "review for others as you would have others review for you".
  • Gerwing 2020 (full text): 1491 comment sets (920 from Publons, 571 from six early-career investigators); fields Ecology and Evolution, and Behavioural Medicine. 12% (179) had at least one unprofessional comment; 41% (611) at least one IIUC; 43% (641) both; 57% (850) neither. Table 1 overall: "Demeaned or Attacked the Author" 12%; "Accusation of Questionable Research Practices" 2%; IIUC items include "Superficial Review" 19%, "Arguments from Authority" 27%. Authors call for a universally agreed reviewer code of conduct.

6. EQUATOR and STROBE

  • EQUATOR: stands for "Enhancing the QUAlity and Transparency Of health Research". Home page says it maintains "a database of reporting guidelines" and offers "Search 700+ reporting guidelines". The word "library" does not appear on the page; "database" is the site's own term (call it the reporting-guideline database/library on slides at your discretion). The page has a section "For peer reviewers".
  • STROBE cross-sectional checklist (strobe-statement.org PDF), exact wording:
    • Item 10 (Study size): "Explain how the study size was arrived at".
    • Item 12 (Statistical methods): "(a) Describe all statistical methods, including those used to control for confounding; (b) Describe any methods used to examine subgroups and interactions; (c) Explain how missing data were addressed; (d) If applicable, describe analytical methods taking account of sampling strategy; (e) Describe any sensitivity analyses". So 12(d) is the clustering/sampling item for cross-sectional studies. E&E example for 12(d) mentions "clustering of participants (e.g., in districts or villages)" and design effect.
    • Item 13 (Participants): "(a) Report numbers of individuals at each stage--eg numbers potentially eligible, examined for eligibility, confirmed eligible, included in the study, completing follow-up, and analysed; (b) Give reasons for non-participation at each stage; (c) Consider use of a flow diagram". Item 6(a) covers eligibility criteria and selection.
    • Item 16 (Main results): "(a) Give unadjusted estimates and, if applicable, confounder-adjusted estimates and their precision (eg, 95% confidence interval). Make clear which confounders were adjusted for and why they were included; (b) Report category boundaries when continuous variables were categorized; (c) ... consider translating estimates of relative risk into absolute risk".
    • Also relevant: item 14(b) "Indicate number of participants with missing data for each variable of interest"; item 19 limitations.
  • Ethics: STROBE has no ethics item (22 items, none on approval or consent). The E&E full text has no ethics discussion (single "ethic" match is a reference title). The ethics-statement standard comes from ICMJE (section 2 above), not STROBE.

7. CLSI and EUCAST versions

  • CLSI M100 page: "36th Edition", "Date of Publication January 26, 2026"; Overview of Changes: "CLSI M100-Ed36 replaces CLSI M100-Ed35, published in 2025." Together with Ed35 (2025) and Ed36 (Jan 2026) this shows a roughly yearly cycle, but the page does not use the word "annual", so "yearly" is my inference from two consecutive editions on this page. Not checked: earlier editions.
  • EUCAST: breakpoint tables page lists "Clinical breakpoints (v 16.1)" valid "24 June 2026 - 31 December 2026" and "Dosages (v 16.0)" dated "1 January 2026". SOP 8.3 section 3.3 "Update frequency": "There is one update released each year in January. Additional updates are normally not provided during the year. A major change or addition during the year is published as a separate addendum." EUCAST citation rule: documents "should be cited in the following way: European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing. Name of document, EUCAST version number, year. Website address."
  • "State the version" is therefore supported for EUCAST (own citation rule requires version number and year) and, by inference, for CLSI (editions replace each other). I found no sentence in either source that literally says "authors must state the version"; that is a reasoned conclusion, not a quote.

Notes per item

1. ICMJE Section V.B (reviewers)

Page heading "B. Use of AI by Reviewers", under "V. Use of Artificial Intelligence in Publishing". The page itself has no date. The ICMJE Recommendations page shows "Updated January 2026". Full section text, verbatim:

"Reviewers must adhere to the journal's stated policy on the use of AI or request permission from the journal prior to using AI technology to facilitate their review. As described in Section II.C.2.c, reviewers must maintain the confidentiality of the manuscript, which may prohibit the uploading of the manuscript to software or other AI technologies where confidentiality cannot be assured unless such use of AI is explicitly permitted by journals. Reviewers who use AI in their review should disclose its use to the journal and ensure the content is appropriate and valid as AI can generate output that can be incorrect, incomplete, or biased."

Mapping: policy/permission = sentence 1; confidentiality/uploading = sentence 2; disclosure and checking output = sentence 3. The source says "adhere to the journal's stated policy ... or request permission", not "and".

V.A (authors), opened: "Authors should carefully review and edit the AI-generated content as the output can be incorrect, incomplete, or biased." Also: "Chatbots (such as ChatGPT) and other AI-assisted tools should not be listed as authors". URL: .../ai-use-by-authors.html.

2. NIH NOT-OD-23-149

Title: "The Use of Generative Artificial Intelligence Technologies is Prohibited for the NIH Peer Review Process". Release date: June 23, 2023. Scope: NIH grant applications and R&D contract proposals (study-section review), NOT journal peer review. Verbatim:

"NIH prohibits NIH scientific peer reviewers from using natural language processors, large language models, or other generative Artificial Intelligence (AI) technologies for analyzing and formulating peer review critiques for grant applications and R&D contract proposals."

"AI tools have no guarantee of where data are being sent, saved, viewed, or used in the future"

"uploading or sharing content or original concepts from an NIH grant application, contract proposal, or critique to online generative AI tools violates the NIH peer review confidentiality and integrity requirements."

Accessibility technologies may get an exception after telling the Designated Federal Officer. The live NIH site gave me a Cloudflare challenge, so the text is from archive.org (snapshot 2026-11-09).

3. Liang et al.

Europe PMC copy of the NEJM AI abstract and arXiv v1 abstract agree.

  • Sample: 3,096 papers in 15 Nature family journals; 1,709 ICLR papers. The NEJM AI version adds 425 health sciences papers (Nature portfolio) and 666 eLife submissions ("similar patterns").
  • Overlap of points, GPT-4 vs human reviewers: 30.85% (Nature), 39.23% (ICLR). Between two human reviewers: 28.58% (Nature), 35.25% (ICLR).
  • arXiv v1 adds: overlap was larger for weaker papers.
  • User study: 308 researchers from 110 institutions (AI and computational biology). 57.4% found the feedback "helpful/very helpful"; 82.4% found it "more beneficial than feedback from at least some human reviewers".
  • Quote (arXiv): "comparable to the overlap between two human reviewers"
  • Caveats: overlap measures shared points, not correctness. Users were AI/computational biology researchers rating feedback on their own papers, not clinicians. I did not read the limitations section; the abstract says only "several limitations".

4. Hosseini and Horbach

Crossref confirms: Res Integr Peer Rev 2023;8:4, published 18 May 2023, DOI 10.1186/s41073-023-00133-5. A correction exists (2023;8:7). From the PMC page (my fetch tool summarised it; quotes are as it returned them, so recheck):

  • Confidentiality: LLMs "may store inputted text"; material "should not be fed into LLMs unless security and data protection measures are put in place".
  • Bias: "some biases such as those related to geography, race or class might be reinforced".
  • Accountability: "Reviewers should disclose the use of LLMs and accept full responsibility" for their reports; human agents should verify accuracy.
  • Also: repeating a prompt can give different output; training for researchers on benefits and risks.

5. Sharma et al.

arXiv 2310.13548 (v4, May 2025). The ICLR 2024 venue comes from the brief; the arXiv page I read does not state it. Quote: "sycophancy is a general behavior of state-of-the-art AI assistants". The paper does not test peer review; any link to review is our inference.

JAMA, BMJ, Sage, IOP: not opened. The Sage page returned 403. Search snippets say Sage and IOP allow AI for language polishing only; unverified, do not cite.

Pattern: Elsevier is strictest (no AI to assist the review); Springer Nature and Wiley allow limited, declared use but bar uploading to public tools. Tell the audience to read the target journal's current policy.

COPE's site lists a forum session called "Artificial intelligence and peer review" ("the state of artificial intelligence (AI) in peer review is discussed"), but I did not open it, so I have no COPE rule on reviewers to quote. Do not attribute a reviewer-AI rule to COPE.

8. Hallucination in AI reviews

Best citable study: Du J et al. LLMs Assist NLP Researchers: Critique Paper (Meta-)Reviewing. EMNLP 2024 main; arXiv 2406.16253. Full-text discussion, verbatim:

"LLMs are more prone to generating contradictory claims in their reviews and making claims that the authors never stated in the submission, indicating a tendency towards hallucination."

Their category "Unstated statement": "Statements made in the review are not supported by content in the paper." Limits: NLP papers, expert-annotated review segments, no medical manuscripts, no rate of fabricated references.

Newer: Lin T-L et al. HalluPeer, arXiv 2609.03580 (EMNLP Findings 2026). I opened the abstract. It is a benchmark with hallucinations injected into reviews; it says "existing detectors struggle to separate hallucinations from legitimate critique". Preprint-level; no rate in the abstract.

Related, abstract opened: Ye et al., arXiv 2412.01708 "Are We There Yet?". Hidden text in manuscripts can manipulate LLM reviews; LLMs were "potentially assigning higher ratings to incomplete papers compared to full papers". This is manipulation and bias, not hallucination.

Fabricated references in review reports: I found no study that tested references inside AI-generated peer review reports. Studies on LLM-generated citations in general exist (for example a neurocritical care study, PMC13506236) but I saw only search snippets, did not open them, and they are not about reviews. Not usable.

What this means for a slide

Safe (opened and checked):

  • ICMJE V.B: follow the journal's AI policy or ask permission; confidentiality may bar uploading; disclose; check output because it "can be incorrect, incomplete, or biased". Cite as ICMJE Recommendations, updated January 2026.
  • NIH bans generative AI in its grant peer review (NOT-OD-23-149, 23 June 2023). Label it as grant review, not journals.
  • Elsevier: no uploading and no AI to assist the review. Springer Nature and Wiley: no uploading to public tools; disclose. Write "accessed Nov 2026".
  • Liang et al. numbers (30.85% vs 28.58%; 57.4%; 82.4%), with the caveats above.
  • Du et al.: LLM reviews contained contradictory and unstated claims (NLP papers).
  • COPE: AI cannot be an author (2023).

Not safe or needs care:

  • Do not say COPE forbids AI in peer review. I found no such COPE text.
  • Do not give a "fabricated reference rate in AI reviews". No review-specific figure found.
  • Do not say "AI review is as good as human review". GPT-4/human overlap is similar to human/human overlap, and both are only about 30% to 39%.
  • Do not use Springer Nature's old "do not upload" wording as current.
  • Sharma et al. is about sycophancy in chat tasks, not review. Present the link as our inference.
  • Hosseini and Horbach: recheck quotes on the PMC page before using quotation marks.
  • Policies change often. The only dates I saw: Elsevier July 2026, ICMJE January 2026.

1. Noble 2017: the ten rules (headings as stated)

  1. Provide an overview, then quote the full set of reviews.
  2. Be polite and respectful of all reviewers.
  3. Accept the blame.
  4. Make the response self-contained.
  5. Respond to every point raised by the reviewer.
  6. Use typography to help the reviewer navigate your response.
  7. Whenever possible, begin your response to each comment with a direct answer to the point being raised.
  8. When possible, do what the reviewer asks.
  9. Be clear about what changed relative to the previous version.
  10. If necessary, write the response twice.

Quotes (checked verbatim against the PLOS full text):

  • Rule 2: "a rude critique does not justify a rude response from you"
  • Rule 5: "you should not try to avoid a difficult point by simply ignoring it"

Other points as stated: Rule 1 says the letter should "contain the complete set of reviews with your responses interleaved". Rule 3 says if the reviewer missed something, apologize and consider revising the text. Rule 9 warns against replying "This point is addressed in the manuscript in the following way", because it does not say whether the text is new. It asks authors to "refer explicitly to the previous and revised versions" and say what changed (it does not say "page/line numbers"). Thanking: the only "thank you" advice is an explicit thank-you in the Acknowledgments for very insightful suggestions; some journals (PLOS Comput Biol included) do not allow naming reviewers.

3. Checklists or tools for appraising AI chatbot output

Several exist. None is a universally accepted standard.

CLEAR tool (Sallam, Barakat, Sallam; Cureus 2023; PMID 38024074). Five items, each scored on a 5-point Likert scale: (1) Is the content sufficient? (2) accurate? (3) evidence-based? (4) clear, concise, and easy to understand? (5) free from irrelevant information? (Themes: Completeness, Lack of false information, Evidence, Appropriateness, Relevance.)

  • Validation as stated: content validity by expert review; pilot with 32 healthcare professionals on 8 topics, Cronbach alpha 0.669-0.981; then two raters scored 4 chatbots on 5 topics, Cohen kappa 0.348 to 0.875.
  • The abstract itself calls for more work: "Future studies are recommended to validate the utility of the CLEAR tool" (paraphrase, not verbatim). So: pilot-validated only.
  • Later papers from the same group (e.g. BMC Infect Dis 2024, PMID 39118057) call it "the validated CLEAR tool"; that is self-description.

QAMAI (Vaira et al., Eur Arch Otorhinolaryngol 2024; PMID 38703195). Six items on 1-5 Likert: accuracy, clarity, relevance, completeness, provision of sources and references, usefulness. Total 6-30 maps to poor / fair / good / very good / excellent. A search summary said it was adapted from mDISCERN; I did not confirm that in the text I read.

  • Validation as reported: 27 reviewers, 25 centres, 30 ChatGPT-4 responses, 792 assessments. One factor explained 51.1% of variance; Cronbach alpha 0.837; ICC 0.983; test-retest r 0.876; CFI 0.989; RMSEA 0.053.
  • Limits: ChatGPT-4 only, head and neck surgery content, single study. Best validated of the three.

METRICS (Sallam et al., Interact J Med Res 2024; PMID 38276872). A reporting checklist for studies of generative AI in health care, not a quality-of-answer score. Nine themes: Model, Evaluation approach, Timing, Transparency of data source, Range of topics, Randomization, Individual factors and inter-rater reliability, Count of queries, Specificity of prompts. Authors call it "preliminary"; applied to 34 records, kappa 0.558-0.962.

DISCERN adapted for chatbots. Appeared only in a web search summary (including a claim about a 2025 meta-synthesis of 42 studies). I did not open any of these papers. Unverified; do not cite without checking.

Plain statement: I found no tool designed or validated for judging AI feedback on a manuscript or a response letter. CLEAR and QAMAI target health information given to patients or clinicians. For the slides, present them as models of structured appraisal and note the gap.

4. Evidence on fabricated or inaccurate AI content

Walters and Wilder, Sci Rep 2023 (abstract read). ChatGPT-3.5 and GPT-4 wrote short literature reviews on 42 multidisciplinary topics: 84 papers, 636 citations. Quote: "55% of the GPT-3.5 citations but just 18% of the GPT-4 citations are fabricated."

  • Of real (non-fabricated) citations, 43% for GPT-3.5 and 24% for GPT-4 "include substantive citation errors". Exact rates: fabricated GPT-3.5 55%, GPT-4 18%; substantive errors in real citations GPT-3.5 43%, GPT-4 24%.
  • Conclusion as stated: "Although GPT-4 is a major improvement over GPT-3.5, problems remain."

Bhattacharyya et al., Cureus 2023 (abstract read). GPT-3.5 wrote 30 short medical papers with 115 references: 47% fabricated, 46% authentic but inaccurate, 7% authentic and accurate. Incorrect PMID in 93% of papers. Medical topics, so closer to the audience; older model.

Liang et al., NEJM AI 2024 (abstract read; my fetch of the abstract was partly garbled). This is about feedback, not citations. GPT-4 comments overlapped with human reviewers: average 28.58% (Nature journals) and 35.25% (ICLR), described as comparable with the overlap between two human reviewers. In a user study of 308 researchers, 57.4% found it helpful or very helpful. The abstract says "We also identify several limitations of large language model (LLM)-generated feedback" but does not list them. A balanced counterpoint: AI feedback can help but does not replace human review.

Caveat: none of these studies tested AI feedback on a response letter. The fabrication studies used 2023 models; rates for current models are not in these sources.

6. Goodman 1994 (abstract read)

Masked before-after study of 111 consecutive original research manuscripts accepted at Annals of Internal Medicine (March 1992 to March 1993). 34-item quality tool, each item 1-5; 44 assessors unaware of the aims; different assessors for the two versions.

  • 33 of 34 items changed in the direction of improvement. Largest gains: discussion of study limitations, generalizations, use of confidence intervals, tone of conclusions.
  • The percentage of items scored 3 or more rose by an absolute 7.3% (95% CI 3.3 to 11.3) from a baseline of 75%. Mean item score rose 0.23 points (CI 0.07 to 0.39) from a baseline of 3.5.
  • Manuscripts in the bottom 50% improved two- to threefold more than the top 50%, after correction for regression to the mean.
  • Quote: "Peer review and editing improve the quality of medical research reporting"
  • Caveats: accepted manuscripts only, one journal, peer review and editing combined (cannot be separated), measures report quality not research quality. A companion JAMA paper (PMID 8015119) used 113 manuscripts; do not mix the two.
