1. What people should leave with

  • Peer review is quality control and improvement, not only gatekeeping.
  • A structured review has four parts: summary, major concerns, minor concerns, recommendation.
  • Critique the work, not the authors: be specific, constructive and evidence-based.
  • AI is good at checklist gaps. It is not a judge of science or truth, and it can be wrong with a confident voice.
  • Never upload a manuscript you are reviewing to a tool that cannot keep it confidential. Follow the journal's AI policy and disclose use.
  • A good response to reviewers answers every comment, shows each change, and disagrees politely with evidence.

This session builds on two earlier ones. At 14:00 the group already gave and received feedback on abstracts. At 15:30 Session 10 (Dr Subodh Gupta) covers why manuscripts get rejected. Do not repeat the list of rejection reasons. Here the audience practises the reviewer's side and the author's reply.

The running example is synthetic. 312 urinary E. coli isolates at a tertiary hospital in Telangana (same example as Session 4). The manuscript excerpt, its seven planted problems and the author replies are written for teaching.

4. Activity: Review this (9–20)

Set-up. Pairs read the excerpt (handout, or slides 11–12) and write a short review on the template: summary, major, minor, recommendation. Eight minutes. Do not tell them how many problems there are.

Answer key: the seven planted problems

#ProblemWhere in the textWhat a good review says
1Unit of analysis unclear"all E. coli isolates", no first-isolate ruleState whether repeat isolates from the same patient were included; use first isolate per patient or account for clustering.
2CLSI/EUCAST version not stated"read using CLSI breakpoints"Name the document and edition; breakpoints change between editions.
3No sample-size justificationNothing in MethodsSay how 312 was chosen, or that it was a census of the year.
4No ethics statementAbsent from the excerptAsk for committee approval and a consent waiver for routine data.
5Causal conclusion from a cross-sectional design"Prior antibiotic use causes ESBL production"Reword to "was associated with"; discuss confounding and temporality.
6Policy recommendation not supported"stop using ceftriaxone"; "first-line … for all UTIs in Telangana"One hospital cannot set state policy; nitrofurantoin does not suit upper-tract infection.
7P values without confidence intervalsaOR 2.4, P < 0.001; aOR 1.7, P = 0.048Report 95% CIs with every OR and percentage.

Genuine problems that were not planted (credit pairs who find them): variable selection by univariable P < 0.2; no per-drug denominators (nitrofurantoin and fosfomycin are often tested on fewer isolates); ceftriaxone resistance (53.8%) is higher than ESBL (50.6%), so ask for a cross-tabulation (AmpC is a possible reason); exposure taken from request forms; "resistance is rising" needs a citation; the identification method is "standard methods". Do not count the missing flow diagram or patient table as errors: it is an excerpt.

Debrief script (2 minutes)

  1. Ask one pair for their summary. Is it one paragraph? Does it say what the paper found, not what they think of it?
  2. Ask which major concern they put first. The causal conclusion usually leads.
  3. Show the key (slide 13). Ask which they missed. Sample size is the one most often missed, by people and by AI.
  4. Ask for one tone check: read a comment aloud that is about the work, not the authors.

5. Human vs AI: what the nine runs showed (20–30)

This is a real test. The same prompt ("You are a peer reviewer. Review this manuscript excerpt.", then the excerpt) was run nine times on 23 November 2026, with no tools and no web: 6 on a small Claude model, 3 on a larger one. The raw outputs are in ai_demo/. Counts are in docs/ai_review_tally.md.

ProblemAI runs that caught it (of 9)
Causal conclusion; policy overreach; repeat isolates; CLSI edition; ethics; P values without CIs9 of 9 each
Sample-size justification1 of 9 (a passing remark). One Opus run called the sample size "reasonable for the question".
Variable selection by P < 0.2 (not planted)9 of 9
Ceftriaxone vs ESBL gap (not planted)6 of 9
AmpC (not planted)5 of 9
A problem that is not there1 of 9: one run said the text mixes "hospitalisation" with "hospitalization". The excerpt has only "hospitalisation".
  • All nine recommended major revision. None flattered the paper. Each opened with a mild positive line and then gave a firm verdict.
  • No reference could be fabricated: the reviews contain no reference lists. Do not tell the room the AI "invents references" in this task.
  • One run assumed what was in the model ("adjusted for only age, sex and hospitalisation"); the excerpt does not say.
  • Several runs asked for things an excerpt cannot hold (a flow diagram, a patient table, an ethics approval number, a limitations section). One run said, "I reviewed only the excerpt provided".
  • Five runs said the percentages agree with n = 312, but the excerpt gives no per-drug denominators. A reviewer can check arithmetic, not truth.

Say clearly: the "model answer" column catches all seven problems by design, so the comparison does not measure human reviewers. Do not conclude that AI is worse than, or as good as, a human. It shows what nine AI reviews looked like.

Published context (abstracts only): GPT-4 and human reviewers raised overlapping points 30.85% (Nature journals) and 39.23% (ICLR) of the time, against 28.58% and 35.25% between two human reviewers; 57.4% of 308 researchers found GPT-4 feedback helpful (Liang et al., NEJM AI 2024). Overlap measures shared points, not correctness. LLM reviews also made contradictory claims and claims the authors never stated (Du et al., EMNLP 2024, NLP papers).

Caveats to say out loud

  • Each call was told to use no tools; other tools, models and days will differ.
  • Counts come from keyword search of the saved files, then reading. They are not a blind rating.
  • Missed by both reviewers: whether the data are real.

6. Rules for AI in peer review (30–36)

Slide 20 quotes 12 words of ICMJE Section V.B: "Reviewers must adhere to the journal's stated policy on the use of AI…". Three ideas in that section: follow the journal's policy or ask permission; the manuscript is confidential and uploading it where confidentiality cannot be assured may be barred; if you use AI, disclose it and check the output, which "can be incorrect, incomplete, or biased". ICMJE says "or", not "and", for policy and permission.

Slide 21: journals differ

  • Elsevier (page updated July 2026): no uploading; AI should not be used to assist the review.
  • Springer Nature: no uploading to unsecured or public AI; disclose any use; AI must not substitute the reviewer's evaluation.
  • Wiley: no uploading manuscripts into GenAI tools; AI used to improve a report must be declared.
  • NIH (June 2023) bans generative AI in grant peer review. It is not a journal rule.
  • All read 23 November 2026. Policies change often. JAMA, BMJ and Sage were not opened.
  • COPE's AI statement (13 February 2023) covers authorship only. Do not say COPE forbids AI in peer review.

Slide 22: in practice

Four cases: read the journal's policy and follow it; if it is silent, ask the editor; do not paste the manuscript into a public chatbot; disclose any AI used for wording or checks. A public chatbot usually cannot assure confidentiality. Some tools have private modes, but only a journal can permit their use.

Slide 23: for authors

Using AI to pre-review your own de-identified draft is legitimate, and the same verification rules apply: check every comment against your manuscript, share no patient identifiers or unpublished data you cannot share, disclose AI use as the journal requires, and stay responsible. ICMJE V.A: "Authors should carefully review and edit the AI-generated content as the output can be incorrect, incomplete, or biased." Detailed ethics and disclosure wording belong to Session 6.

7. Responding to reviewers (36–41)

  • Thank the editor and reviewers; answer every comment and never ignore one; quote the comment then reply; show each change with page and line numbers; disagree politely, with evidence.
  • Sources read: Noble, PLoS Comput Biol 2017 (full text); the Frontiers and PLOS author guides (web pages, not peer-reviewed); Williams, J Am Acad Dermatol 2004 (abstract only). Noble has no page-and-line rule and no thank-you rule; "line numbers" is in the PLOS guide. Page numbers are common practice, not found in these sources.

Slide 26: one good, one poor

Reviewer comment: the conclusion says prior antibiotic use "causes" ESBL production. Poor: "The reviewer is wrong. Everyone knows antibiotics cause resistance. No change made." It is hostile, skips the point, shows no change. Good: thanks, agrees, changes "causes" to "was associated with" in the Abstract and Conclusions, adds a limitations paragraph, and gives page and line numbers. The page and line numbers are invented for teaching.

Slide 27: when you disagree

Reviewer 2 asks for resistance by infection type. The reply explains the request forms did not record it, states this as a limitation, and suggests future work. The missing infection type is a made-up reason, consistent with the excerpt. If you cannot do something, say why and show what you did instead.

9. Questions you may get

Can I use ChatGPT to help review a paper?

Only if the journal's policy says so, or the editor permits it. Never paste the manuscript into a tool that cannot keep it confidential. If you do use AI, disclose it and check every point yourself. Read the journal's current policy: Elsevier bars AI help; Springer Nature and Wiley allow limited, declared use but not uploading to public tools.

Is an AI review as good as a human review?

We cannot say from this session. In published work GPT-4 and human reviewers raised overlapping points about as often as two humans did, but overlap is not correctness. In the nine runs here, AI caught the planted reporting gaps and missed the sample-size justification in 8 of 9.

Did the AI invent references?

Not in this task: the reviews have no reference lists. In other tasks it can (published rates from older models are in the Session 7 deck). Check every claim and citation.

What if I disagree with a reviewer who is wrong?

Say so politely and give evidence: data, a guideline, a citation. Show what you changed or why you did not. Never ignore the comment.

How many reviewers and how long should a review be?

It depends on the journal. Follow the review form. Order major concerns by importance. A review is as long as it needs to be to be specific.

Do reviewers catch most errors?

Not reliably. In a BMJ test (Schroter 2008, abstract only), 607 reviewers found on average 2.6 of 9 inserted major errors, and training had small effects. Peer review still improves reports: in a masked before-and-after study of 111 accepted manuscripts, 33 of 34 quality items improved (Goodman 1994, abstract only). That is why a structure and a checklist help.

What does COPE say about reviewers?

Version 1 (March 2013) covers confidentiality, conflicts of interest, timeliness, expertise and tone. The 2017 version was not opened for this deck, so check publicationethics.org before quoting. COPE's 2023 statement on AI covers authorship only; it says nothing about reviewers.

Can I reuse a reviewer's comments in my own work?

No. Reviews are confidential unless the journal publishes them (open peer review).

COPE Ethical Guidelines for Peer Reviewers: only v1 (March 2013) was read, from mirror copies, because the COPE site blocks automated access. v2 (2017) was not opened, so no v2 wording is on a slide; open the official PDF in a browser before quoting COPE.
